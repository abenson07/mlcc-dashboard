# Event copy — MLCC context, voice & tone

Use everything below when drafting event descriptions and related copy. **Master context** explains who MLCC is; **voice & tone** defines how to sound.

---

## Master context — Maple Leaf Community Council (MLCC)

### 1. What MLCC is

The Maple Leaf Community Council (MLCC) is a Seattle-based neighborhood nonprofit / community organization focused on strengthening local connection, civic engagement, and neighborhood quality of life in Maple Leaf.

MLCC operates as both:

- A community-facing organization (events, meetings, communications, outreach)
- A volunteer-driven institution with board roles, committees, and recurring operational work

There is a strong emphasis on:

- Inclusivity
- Local participation
- Visible, tangible neighborhood impact
- Revitalizing and sustaining long-running neighborhood traditions while bringing in new people

### 2. Core activities & programs

**Recurring & signature events**

MLCC runs a mix of social, civic, and cultural events, including:

- **Movie nights** — Multiple events per year; planning, setup, teardown, and volunteer staffing; community-building and visibility.
- **Silent Book Club** — Monthly, ongoing; low-pressure, inclusive gathering; consistent engagement touchpoint. In Maple Leaf it meets on the **third Sunday of each month** (unless a specific event says otherwise).
- **Love Your Neighbor Happy Hour** — Social connection–focused; multi-person planning; designed to lower barriers to participation.
- **Summer Social** — Larger anchor event; months of lead-up; high volunteer involvement on the day; flagship community moment.
- **Halloween Parade** — Logistically complex; planning, setup, breakdown, and day-of coordination; family-friendly, tradition-oriented.
- **Community meetings** — Civic-oriented; planning and facilitation; smaller volunteer groups with higher responsibility.

### 3. Communications & outreach

**Newsletter**

- A major operational pillar; produced multiple times per year (at least four issues referenced).
- Requires roughly ~20 hours per issue across multiple volunteers.
- Central channel for event promotion, organizational updates, volunteer recognition, and long-term storytelling about MLCC’s role in the neighborhood.

**Distribution / routes**

- Physical distribution routes (approximately 50); each route ~1 hour; delivered by volunteers.
- Strong emphasis on offline, neighborhood-first communication, not only digital.

**Broader comms strategy**

- MLCC messaging balances heritage and continuity (“this institution has always been here”) with renewal and momentum (“new people, new energy, new systems”).
- Communications are often framed as: what’s improving, how to get involved, and (when useful) the system behind the scenes.

### 4. Organizational structure

**Board & leadership**

- MLCC has a formal board structure.
- Board work includes governance, financial oversight, strategic planning, and volunteer coordination.

**Committees**

Known committees include:

- **Communications Committee** — Voice & tone definition; messaging alignment; newsletter, blog, and outreach strategy.
- **Outreach Committee** — Small community gatherings, community meetings, Silent Book Club, grassroots engagement.

Committees are volunteer-led, semi-autonomous, and coordinated through shared goals rather than rigid hierarchy.

### 5. Volunteers

**Scale & nature**

- Dozens of volunteers across events and logistics; often episodic participation and multiple hats.
- Volunteer labor is a critical asset, not incidental.

**Tracking & reporting**

- Volunteer hours matter for grants, reporting, board storytelling, and demonstrating impact.

### 6. Digital & technical ecosystem

- **Website** — Webflow; public hub, membership entry point, event and committee content; clear CTAs, improved IA, stronger committee sections.
- **Membership system** — In progress: database, roles, renewals, CSV uploads, manual entry; stack includes Supabase, Stripe, and potential QuickBooks tie-ins.
- **Finance & admin** — Interest in clean reporting and simple mental models for board visibility.

### 7. Tone, values, and philosophy

**Organizational voice**

- Friendly, neighborly, human — not corporate or sterile.
- Serious about civic responsibility without being bureaucratic.

**Values**

- Belonging over scale
- Participation over perfection
- Transparency over polish
- Momentum over stagnation

MLCC is framed less as a service provider and more as a shared civic project.

### 8. Strategic themes

1. **Institutional continuity** — Long-standing neighborhood institution; stewardship mindset.
2. **Renewal & growth** — New volunteers; lower barriers; modernize tools without losing soul.
3. **Operational clarity** — Clear roles, better systems, less cognitive load for volunteers and board.
4. **Visibility of impact** — Volunteer hours, events executed, outcomes made legible.

## Voice & tone guide

### Purpose

This guide defines how the Maple Leaf Community Council (MLCC) communicates across channels — including email, social media, blog posts, event announcements, and community updates.

Its goal is to ensure MLCC’s communications feel consistent, human, and trustworthy, while remaining accessible to a wide range of neighbors with different levels of engagement.

### 1. Voice vs. tone (how to use this guide)

- **Voice** is consistent. It reflects who MLCC is.
- **Tone** adapts based on context, topic, and audience.

This guide defines a single, stable voice with intentional tone shifts depending on situation.

### 2. Core voice

MLCC’s voice is **neighborly, clear, and welcoming**.

We sound like people who live in the neighborhood, care about it deeply, and want others to feel comfortable getting involved — at any level.

We do not lead with authority, credentials, or formality. We lead with shared place and shared care.

In practice:

- Writing in plain language
- Favoring warmth over polish
- Prioritizing clarity over cleverness
- Choosing invitation over instruction

### 3. Voice attributes

These traits should be present in nearly all MLCC communications.

**Neighborly**

We speak as part of the community, not above it.

- Use inclusive language (“we,” “our neighborhood”).
- Avoid distancing language (“the council,” “residents are encouraged…”).
- Assume shared context whenever possible.

**Welcoming**

Our default posture is open and low-pressure.

- Make participation feel optional, not obligatory.
- Reduce intimidation for first-time readers or attendees.
- Avoid insider jargon or unexplained acronyms.

**Clear**

We respect people’s time and attention.

- Be concise.
- Lead with what matters.
- Avoid unnecessary procedural detail unless required.

**Informative (without being heavy)**

We share what people need to know — not everything we know.

- Prioritize relevance over completeness.
- Give enough context to understand “why.”
- Link out or offer follow-ups for deeper detail.

### 4. Tone spectrum

MLCC’s tone lives in a balanced middle ground:

- Casual, but not sloppy
- Friendly, but not flippant
- Organized, but not bureaucratic
- Calm, but not passive

Tone should never feel alarmist, scolding, performative, or overly official.

### 5. Contextual tone shifts

While the voice stays the same, tone should adjust slightly depending on context.

**Everyday updates & events**

- Tone: friendly, light, conversational.
- Goal: make participation feel easy and normal.

**Planning, process, or governance topics**

- Tone: calm, neutral, respectful.
- Goal: build trust through clarity and steadiness.
- Avoid over-formal distance; avoid over-simplifying complex issues into vagueness.

**Sensitive topics (zoning, safety, conflict)**

- Tone: measured, thoughtful, extra clear.
- Goal: inform without inflaming; acknowledge impact; avoid taking sides unless explicitly required.
- Emojis and playful language should generally be avoided here.

### 6. Framing & structure

**Preferred message structure**

Most communications should follow:

1. What’s happening
2. Why it matters to neighbors
3. How to engage (optional, not mandatory)

**Short descriptions (Webflow / event cards)**

- **Length:** Aim for about **72 characters** (stay roughly **65–78**). This line appears under the title in listings and must read cleanly on one line on most screens.
- **Job:** One crisp beat: time pattern, place, or who it’s for — **not** a paragraph. Avoid repeating the full title if the title already states the program name and month or year.
- **Voice:** Neighborly, plain language, invitation not command. No em dash habit, no hard sell.

**Headlines & openers**

Headlines should feel human, skimmable, and relevant to daily neighborhood life.

Avoid agenda-style titles, overly formal announcements, and urgency without explanation.

**Titles for recurring & signature programs**

When the event is one of these programs, put the **time cue first** in the public title (and in short descriptions when the title is repeated or echoed), so neighbors know *which* edition it is:

- **Silent Book Club** — Use the **month** of the gathering (e.g., *May Silent Book Club*).
- **Community meeting** — Use the **season**, usually *Spring* or *Fall* (e.g., *Spring Community Meeting*).
- **Summer Social** and **Movie nights** — Use the **calendar year** (e.g., *2026 Summer Social*).

Derive month, season, and year from the **confirmed schedule** in the brief or form; do not guess a month, season, or year that the facts don’t support.

**Monthly Silent Book Club (series copy)**

- **Rhythm:** The neighborhood Silent Book Club follows the **third Sunday of each month**. Say so plainly when you describe the series or a single month’s gathering, so new people can remember the pattern.
- **Month in the title:** Keep using the month in the public title (see above), e.g., *September Silent Book Club*.
- **Fresh angles every month:** Avoid recycling the same hook as last month or the AI default. Take a **seasonal or monthly** angle: weather in Seattle, school-year rhythm, holidays, or one light line of wordplay. Examples of the *kind* of angle (adapt, don’t paste): stepping out of **August** heat into a cool hour of reading; **fall** wordplay like “fall into a good chapter”; **December** coziness before the holiday rush; **January** as a gentle reset; **spring** as lighter evenings and getting outside later. Stay **one notch** below “marketing campaign”: neighborly, specific, skimmable.
- **Guardrails:** Still plain-spoken MLCC voice: welcoming, low-pressure, inclusive. No guilt trips, no hard sell. One seasonal line is plenty; the rest should be concrete: what happens, when, where, who it’s for.
- **Short description on cards:** For Silent Book Club listings, the **teaser line** should still hit the **~72 character** card guideline (see **Short descriptions** above). The title already carries the month; the teaser can lean on third Sunday, Watershed, or one seasonal hook — not all three every time.

### 7. Language guidelines

**Do**

- Use plain, everyday language.
- Write as if speaking to one person.
- Favor active voice.
- Keep sentences short to medium length.
- Assume good intent from readers.

**Avoid**

- Bureaucratic phrasing.
- Corporate or nonprofit jargon.
- Excessive urgency.
- All-caps emphasis.
- Language that sounds like enforcement or compliance.

### 8. Emoji & expressiveness

Emojis are allowed, not required. Use them when they add warmth, match the subject, or reflect community moments (events, seasons, gatherings).

- One emoji is usually enough.
- Avoid emoji strings.
- Avoid emojis in sensitive or serious communications.

### 9. Emotional outcome

After reading MLCC communications, neighbors should feel:

- Informed, not overwhelmed
- Welcomed, not pressured
- Respected, not managed
- Encouraged to participate on their own terms

### 10. Final quality check

Before publishing, ask:

- Does this sound like it came from someone who lives here?
- Would this feel welcoming to someone brand new?
- Is the tone calm, clear, and human?
- Are we informing and inviting — not directing?
- Avoid AI-identifying habits: do not lean on em dashes (—) as a default punctuation habit or overuse bulleted lists; prefer natural prose where it fits.
- Keep language at about a **9th-grade reading level**: simple, clear, and easy to skim.

If the answer is yes, the message is on voice.
